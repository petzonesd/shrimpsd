Cloudy water in a shrimp tank

What the color of the haze tells you, which kinds are harmless, and how to clear it without stressing your shrimp.

The short answer

In one line

Cloudy water is usually a sign of extra nutrients or fine particles, not an emergency; find the cause, cut back the source, and fix it with patience and small water changes rather than one big one.

The color of the cloud is your best clue. Milky white points to a bacterial bloom, green points to floating algae, brown is tannins from wood or leaves, and a gray or tan dust is usually particles stirred up from substrate. Shrimp are sensitive to sudden changes, so the gentle route nearly always beats the dramatic one.

Milky white water

A white or grayish haze is most often a bacterial bloom. Free-floating bacteria multiply when there is extra food in the water, such as uneaten food, a dead shrimp, decaying plant matter, or the early ammonia of a tank that is still cycling. It is common in the first weeks of a new setup; see cycling a shrimp tank. Remove anything rotting, feed less, and let the filter run. In most tanks it fades by itself as the bacteria settle onto surfaces. If it appears suddenly in an established tank, look for a hidden casualty and read why shrimp die.

Green water

Green, pea-soup water is a bloom of free-floating algae. It needs light and nutrients, so the usual triggers are a tank near a sunny window, lights left on too long, and overfeeding. Move the tank out of direct sun, shorten the lighting period, and cut back on food. Our lighting guide and algae guide cover the details. Shrimp generally tolerate green water well and will even graze on it, so there is no need to rush.

Brown, tea-colored water

Yellow to brown water comes from tannins released by driftwood, catappa leaves, alder cones and similar botanicals. It is harmless and many keepers like it; see catappa leaves and botanicals. If you prefer clear water, small regular water changes will lighten it, and pre-soaking new wood helps.

Dust and fine particles

New substrate, especially if it was not rinsed, can cloud a tank for a day or two when it is first filled. Pour water slowly onto a plate or bowl instead of straight onto the substrate. Active substrates meant for Caridina can release fine particles for longer; see substrate for shrimp. A healthy filter will usually clear this on its own.

How to clear it

The steps are the same whatever the color, though the cause you remove will differ.

  1. Check the shrimp and the basics. Look at how the shrimp are behaving and test ammonia and nitrite if you can. Check the temperature.
  2. Work out what kind of cloud it is. Milky white, green, brown, or fine dust each point to a different cause.
  3. Remove the likely cause. Take out uneaten food and dead plant or animal matter, cut back feeding, and shorten the lighting period if the water is green.
  4. Leave the filter running. Do not turn it off or wash the sponge in tap water.
  5. Change a small amount of treated water if needed. Use treated water matched for temperature and TDS, and keep the change small.
  6. Wait and observe. Most blooms clear over days. Avoid adding new shrimp until the water is clear and the readings are stable.

For how to match new water and keep changes small, see water changes. On our San Diego tap, treat every batch for chloramine first; see chloramine and shrimp.

What not to do

Do not switch the filter off, do not wash the sponge or media under tap water, do not dose medications or clarifiers you have not researched (some contain copper or other compounds that can harm invertebrates; see medications and copper), and do not drain the tank. If shrimp are gasping or dying, test the water and consider extra aeration while you work out the cause.

Questions we get asked

Is cloudy water dangerous to shrimp?

Not always. Milky bacterial blooms and green water are usually survivable, but they show the tank has extra nutrients, so check ammonia and nitrite. Brown tea-colored water from wood or leaves is harmless. Sudden cloudiness together with dead or gasping shrimp needs a test and a gentle water change.

Why is my new shrimp tank cloudy?

A milky haze in a new tank is usually a bacterial bloom, where bacteria multiply on the first food and organic waste in the water. It tends to fade as the tank settles. Dust from new substrate can also cloud the water for a day or two.

Should I do a big water change to clear cloudy water?

No. A large change can swing temperature, TDS and pH and stress shrimp. Find the cause first, then use small, regular changes with treated water if you need them.

How do I clear green water?

Green water is a free-floating algae bloom, usually from strong light or direct sun plus extra nutrients. Cut the lighting period, move the tank out of direct sun, stop overfeeding, and keep the filter running. It usually clears over days to weeks.

Is brown water from driftwood or leaves harmful?

No. The tea color comes from tannins released by wood, catappa leaves or alder cones. It is harmless to shrimp and tends to lower pH slightly, which Caridina keepers often welcome. Regular small water changes lighten it if you prefer clear water.

Should I turn the filter off when the water is cloudy?

No. Leave the filter running so beneficial bacteria stay alive and oxygenated. Do not rinse the sponge in tap water, because chloramine would harm those bacteria.
