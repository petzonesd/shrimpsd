Catappa leaves and botanicals for shrimp

Leaves, cones and porous wood give shrimp something to graze and somewhere to hide. Here is what they do, what they do not do, and how to use them sensibly.

The short answer

In one line

Botanicals are a food-and-shelter extra, not a water fix: they feed the biofilm shrimp graze on, they give cover, and they are best added a little at a time.

As catappa leaves, alder cones and similar botanicals soften in water, bacteria and microscopic life colonise them. Shrimp pick over that film all day, and baby shrimp in particular benefit from having it spread around the tank. Pieces of leaf and cone also give shy shrimp cover, which is useful after a molt. The same ideas appear in our feeding guide.

What each one is for

Catappa (Indian almond) leaves are the usual starting point. They float at first, sink as they soak, and break down over a few weeks. Alder cones are small, hold their shape longer, and are popular in breeding tanks where keepers want a lot of tiny grazing surfaces. Cholla wood is porous and hollow, so shrimp can climb through it and graze it, and it softens slowly. All three release tannins, which tint the water a pale tea colour.

What they do not do

Tannins are sometimes said to lower pH and soften water. In practice any effect is small, and in a tank filled from San Diego tap water, which is hard and well buffered, you should not expect botanicals to change the numbers. Caridina keepers still need soft, mineral-controlled water and the right substrate; leaves are a supplement, not a replacement. Botanicals also do not replace a balanced diet, and they are not a treatment for sick shrimp.

How to use them

Buy products sold for aquarium use. Rinse leaves, cones or wood briefly, and soak them in a separate container until they sink, which also lets the first heavy burst of tannins out. Start with one leaf or a small handful of cones in a small tank and watch the water and the shrimp for a few days before adding more. Keep the amount modest, because decaying plant matter adds to the waste load just as extra food does; the same caution applies as in water changes and when you are cycling a new tank.

Remove a leaf when it is mostly gone or looks slimy, and take out any cone or piece of wood that smells or grows a thick grey film. A little white fuzz on new pieces is common and usually fades as shrimp and snails graze it.

Questions we get asked

Do catappa leaves lower pH in a shrimp tank?

They release tannins that tint the water, but the effect on pH is small and should not be relied on. In hard, well-buffered tap water it is usually negligible. Caridina setups need proper soft-water and buffering-substrate management instead.

Do shrimp eat catappa leaves?

They graze on the biofilm and microbes that grow on the leaf surface and on the softened leaf as it breaks down. Many keepers see shrimp crowd onto a leaf within hours or days of adding it.

How often should I replace botanicals?

Replace a leaf once it is mostly broken down or falling apart, which commonly takes a few weeks. Remove anything that looks slimy or foul rather than waiting for it to disappear.

Will botanicals make my water brown?

Often a little. The tint is from tannins and is generally harmless to shrimp. Soaking leaves in a separate container first reduces it, and a normal small water change clears it.

Are all leaves and cones safe for shrimp?

No. Use products sold for aquarium use. Leaves, cones or wood collected outdoors can carry pesticides, pollution or compounds that harm shrimp, so we would not use them.
