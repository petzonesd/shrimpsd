Troubleshooting

Why are my shrimp dying?

The question we hear most at the counter. The good news: when shrimp die, the timing usually points straight at the cause. Work out when the losses started and the list gets short fast.

By the shrimp desk at Pet Zone Convoy, San Diego · Updated October 8, 2026

Do this in the next hour

Triage first, diagnose second

Remove any dead shrimp. Stop feeding for a day. Test ammonia, nitrite, GH and TDS. Write down everything that went into the tank in the last week — food, fertilizer, medication, new plants, a new decoration, a top-off. If ammonia or nitrite reads above zero, do a small water change (10–20%) with water matched to your tank’s TDS and temperature. Do not do a big change in a panic; a sudden swing kills more shrimp than the problem it is meant to fix.

When did the deaths start?

Within 1–14 days of buyingAlmost always acclimation or the tank itself. Rushed drip, a big TDS gap between the shop water and yours, or a tank that was not fully cycled. How to acclimate shrimp.
A day or two after a water changeThe new water was different — untreated chloramine, a different TDS or GH, or a temperature gap. Or the change was simply too big.
Soon after dosing somethingCopper or another toxin. Check the label of every medication, fertilizer and “plant food” for copper.
Soon after adding plantsPesticide residue. Common on potted plants not grown for shrimp tanks.
One every few days, for weeksMolting trouble from the wrong or unstable GH, a slow-building water quality problem, or simply old age in an older colony.
Many at once, overnightA toxin or an oxygen crash — heat, a dead filter, a spray used in the room, or a large rotting meal.

The ten causes we see most

1. Acclimation shock

Shrimp handle a wide range of water and very little sudden change in it. A fast move from one TDS to another disrupts molting, and the damage shows up days later, which is why people blame the shop or the shrimp. Drip slowly, for one to two hours with Neocaridina and two to three with Caridina.

2. Failed molts (the “white ring”)

A shrimp that dies part way out of its old shell, or with a pale band between the head and body, could not finish molting. The usual causes are GH outside the target range or a sudden change in it. Neocaridina want GH 6–8 (tolerating to about 12); Caridina want GH 4–6. Stable matters more than perfect. See the water parameter reference.

3. Untreated chloramine

San Diego tap water is treated with chloramine, not just chlorine. Leaving a bucket out overnight does nothing to it. Every drop of tap water, including top-offs, needs a conditioner that handles chloramine, dosed for the volume you are adding. More on San Diego tap water for shrimp.

4. Copper

Shrimp are far more sensitive to copper than fish. It shows up in many general fish medications, some plant fertilizers and algae treatments. If a product does not state that it is invertebrate safe, read the ingredient list before it goes anywhere near a shrimp tank.

5. An uncycled or unstable tank

Ammonia and nitrite at any reading above zero hurt shrimp. A new tank needs to be fully cycled, and ideally running for a month or more, before shrimp go in. The shrimp tank setup guide covers the order.

6. Pesticides on new plants

Plants grown for ponds, gardens or general retail are sometimes treated with insecticides, and shrimp are insects’ close relatives. Tissue-culture (in-vitro) plants are the safest choice. Otherwise rinse well and hold new plants in a separate container for a couple of weeks before they go in.

7. Overfeeding

Uneaten food rots, and in a small tank that quickly becomes an ammonia spike. Feed an amount the colony finishes in about two to three hours, skip a day or two a week, and remove leftovers.

8. Heat

Most San Diego rooms suit shrimp year round, but inland heat waves can push a tank well past 80°F, where oxygen drops and stress climbs. Caridina feel it first. A fan across the water surface and extra surface agitation help on hot days.

9. Something in the air

Bug sprays, air fresheners, cleaning sprays and paint fumes land on the water surface and are pulled in by an air pump. If a whole colony died overnight with nothing changed in the tank, ask what was sprayed in the room.

10. Predators and old age

A colony that never grows usually has a fish eating the babies. And Neocaridina live roughly one to two years, so an occasional single death in an established colony that is still breeding is normal. A pattern is not.

How to tell if the colony is healthy

Shrimp that are fine graze constantly, molt every few weeks (you will find empty shells, which you can leave in to be eaten), carry eggs, and spread out across the tank. Shrimp that are struggling hide, sit still, swim frantically around the tank after a water change, or lose color. Frantic swimming right after a change is a warning to slow down, not a sign that they are happy.

Questions we get asked

Why are my shrimp dying one by one?

A slow drip of losses over weeks usually means molting trouble from GH that is wrong or unstable, a low-level water quality problem, or old age in an older colony. Test GH and TDS, keep water changes small and matched, and check that the colony is still breeding.

Why did my new shrimp die after a few days?

Almost always the speed of the change during acclimation, or a tank that was not fully cycled. The damage shows up days later. Drip acclimate for one to two hours for Neocaridina and two to three for Caridina, into a tank that has been running steadily for a month or more.

What is the white ring of death in shrimp?

A pale band between the head and body that shows a shrimp could not finish molting. It is a sign the GH is outside the shrimp’s range or changed suddenly. Correct it slowly with small water changes rather than all at once.

Can tap water kill shrimp?

Untreated San Diego tap water can, because it contains chloramine, which does not evaporate when the water stands. Treat every drop of tap water, including top-offs, with a conditioner that handles chloramine.

Should I do a big water change if my shrimp are dying?

No. A large change is a sudden swing in itself and often makes things worse. Do small changes of 10 to 20 percent with water matched to the tank’s TDS and temperature, and fix the underlying cause.
