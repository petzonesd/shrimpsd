How to tell male from female shrimp

Size, body shape, colour and the saddle are the main clues. Adults are straightforward; young shrimp take time.

The short answer

In one line

Adult females are usually bigger, rounder and more colourful; males are smaller and slimmer. A saddle or eggs means female. Young shrimp cannot be sexed reliably.

Telling the sexes apart is easiest in a group, because you can compare shrimp side by side. A single shrimp on its own is much harder to judge. None of the clues below is perfect by itself, so use several together.

What to look for

Size. Adult females are usually noticeably larger than adult males of the same age and line.

Body shape. Females tend to have a deeper, more curved underside to the abdomen, and a broader tail fan that can cover eggs. Males are slimmer and straighter along the underside.

Colour. In many colour lines, females show richer, fuller colour and males look paler or more transparent. This is a helpful hint rather than a rule, see red cherry shrimp for a line where it is easy to see.

The saddle. A yellow or green patch on the back behind the head, visible through the shell, is the ovary developing. Only maturing females have one. Males never do.

Eggs. A shrimp carrying eggs under her abdomen is berried, and is always female. See berried shrimp and breeding for what happens next.

When you can tell

Juveniles of both sexes look alike. The differences appear gradually as the shrimp grow, usually over a few months, and faster in warm, well-fed tanks than in cool or sparse ones. Do not worry if your young shrimp seem to have no clear sex yet. Give them time, and keep them well fed and in stable water, see feeding shrimp and water parameters.

Amano shrimp

Amano shrimp are the same in broad terms: females are larger. The spots along the side of the body are often cited as another clue, with females showing longer dashes and males showing smaller round dots, but this is a tendency, so check size too. Amano females can carry eggs at home but the larvae need brackish water to develop, so they are not bred in a home tank, see Amano shrimp.

Do you need to sort them?

For most keepers, not really. A mixed group of adults will breed when the tank is stable, and you do not need to count sexes or keep a set ratio. Sexing matters mainly if you are selecting for colour, or if you are curious why a colony is not breeding yet. If you are starting out, see how many shrimp per gallon and choose a group of healthy adults, see how to choose healthy shrimp.

Questions we get asked

How can I tell if my shrimp is male or female?

Look at size and shape first. Females are usually larger, with a deeper, rounder underside and a broader tail fan, while males are smaller and slimmer. Females are also often more strongly coloured. A mature female may show a saddle, and a berried shrimp carrying eggs is always female.

What is a shrimp saddle?

The saddle is a yellowish or greenish patch on the back just behind the head, seen through the shell. It is the developing ovaries, so it appears only in females that are maturing, and it is often a sign that a female is close to being berried.

Can I sex baby shrimp?

Not reliably. Juveniles of both sexes look alike, and the differences in size, shape and colour only become clear as they mature, which takes a few months depending on temperature and food.

Do I need males and females to breed shrimp?

Yes. A mixed group of adults will breed on its own when conditions suit them, so there is no need to pair shrimp or count sexes if you start with a decent-sized group of adults.

Are male shrimp less colourful?

In many colour lines, yes. Females tend to show deeper, fuller colour, and males are often paler and see-through. In a line such as Red Cherry this is the quickest visual clue, though how strong it is varies with the line and the individual.
