Planaria and hydra in a shrimp tank

Most tiny critters in a shrimp tank are harmless. Planaria and hydra are the two worth knowing, mainly because of baby shrimp.

The short answer

In one line

Cut the food supply, siphon debris, rinse and quarantine new plants, and be cautious with any treatment, because many products that kill pests also harm shrimp.

A few small worms or specks are not an emergency. A growing population is a sign the tank has more food and waste than the shrimp are clearing, and that is the thing to fix first.

How to tell what you have

Planaria are flatworms. They are flat, often brownish or whitish, with a slightly triangular head, and they glide along glass and decor rather than wriggle. They tend to come out at night or when you add food.

Hydra are very small, often only a few millimetres, with a stalk and a ring of thin tentacles. They stick to glass, plants and decor and may look like tiny sprigs of green or clear hair that contract when touched.

Harmless look-alikes include copepods and thin detritus worms. They show up in tanks with plenty of food and waste and do not hunt shrimp. If you cannot tell, take a clear photo and bring it by, see why shrimp hide for other normal behavior.

Why they matter

Adult shrimp are rarely the target, but baby shrimp are small enough to be caught. If a colony that was breeding well has stopped producing survivors, pests are one of several possible causes, see breeding and berried shrimp and why shrimp die.

Where they come from

They usually arrive as hitchhikers on plants, moss, decor or in water from another tank, then multiply on leftover food. Rinse new plants, and quarantine plants and new shrimp before they join the main tank, see quarantining new shrimp and plants for shrimp.

What to do first

Feed less. Give only what the shrimp finish in a few hours and remove the rest, see feeding shrimp. Overfeeding is the most common reason pest numbers climb.

Clean up. Siphon debris from the substrate during regular water changes, and wipe or scrape hydra from the glass where you can reach them.

Trap planaria. A small piece of food in a simple trap left overnight and removed in the morning can pull out a good number of flatworms. Repeat for several nights.

Treatments: go carefully

Some products sold for planaria and hydra are marketed as aquarium dewormers. Many aquarium treatments are harmful to shrimp and snails, and copper is especially dangerous, see medications and copper. Do not add anything without reading the label for invertebrate safety. If a tank is badly infested, the safest approach may be moving the shrimp to a clean tank first. Ask us in store before you buy a treatment, because we would rather help you choose carefully than have you lose a colony.

Questions we get asked

What are the small white worms in my shrimp tank?

Tiny thin white worms on the glass are often harmless detritus worms or copepods that thrive when there is extra food and waste in the tank. Planaria are flatworms with a flat, slightly triangular head and a gliding movement, and they are the ones worth acting on. If you are unsure which you have, bring a photo or a sample to the store.

Do planaria and hydra kill adult shrimp?

Adult shrimp are rarely a target, though a stressed or freshly molted shrimp may be at risk. The bigger concern is baby shrimp, which are small enough for hydra to catch and planaria to attack. A tank with a breeding colony is where they matter most.

Where do planaria and hydra come from?

They usually arrive as tiny hitchhikers on plants, moss, decor or in water from another tank, and they multiply when there is leftover food and waste to feed on. Rinsing new plants and quarantining them first helps keep them out.

What is the safest first step against planaria?

Feed less, remove uneaten food after a few hours, and siphon debris from the substrate. Many outbreaks fade once the extra food supply is cut. You can also use a simple baited trap and remove it with the flatworms in it.

Can I use a dewormer or medication in a shrimp tank?

Be careful. Many aquarium treatments are harmful to shrimp and snails, and copper is especially dangerous. Do not add any product before reading its label for invertebrate safety and talking to someone who keeps shrimp, and consider moving the shrimp to a clean tank first.
