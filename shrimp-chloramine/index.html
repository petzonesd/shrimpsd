Chloramine and shrimp

What is in San Diego tap water, and how to make it safe for a shrimp tank.

The short answer

In one line

San Diego tap water contains chloramine, which does not leave a bucket by standing, so treat every batch with a conditioner that neutralizes chloramine before it touches the tank.

Many cities used to disinfect with free chlorine, which gasses off if water sits. San Diego uses chloramine, a chlorine and ammonia compound that is far more stable. That is good for the pipes and not good for a shrimp tank: it is toxic to shrimp and to the bacteria in your filter. For Neocaridina and Amano the fix is simple and cheap. For Caridina, tap water is the wrong starting point anyway; see RO water for shrimp.

What is in San Diego water

The City of San Diego reports an average chloramine residual of about 1.75 ppm in its 2025 water quality report, alongside hardness of roughly 237 to 243 ppm and a pH near 8.3. The full picture is on our San Diego tap water page. Residual levels vary by neighborhood and over time, so treat the water the same way every time rather than guessing.

Why standing water does not work

The old advice to leave a bucket out overnight only works for free chlorine. Chloramine stays dissolved, and an air stone does not remove it in any practical time. A bucket that has stood for a day is still chloraminated.

How to treat the water

Use a water conditioner that states it neutralizes chloramine, and treat the new water before it goes in, not after.

  1. Measure the new water. Work out how much water you are adding, not the size of the whole tank.
  2. Add a conditioner that neutralizes chloramine. Dose it for that volume of water, following the product label, and mix it in the bucket.
  3. Match temperature and TDS. Check the new water against the tank with a thermometer and a TDS meter before it goes in.
  4. Add it slowly. Pour or drip it in gently so the tank does not swing. Never add untreated tap water directly to the tank.

This fits into the routine on our water changes guide. Keep changes small, because a conditioner fixes the chloramine but not a large swing in temperature or TDS.

What happens to the ammonia

Chloramine is bound to ammonia, so breaking it apart releases some. Some conditioners temporarily bind that ammonia into a less harmful form, and some ammonia test kits can still show a reading afterward. In a cycled tank with a healthy colony of filter bacteria this is usually handled; see cycling a shrimp tank. If your readings are high and shrimp look unwell, change a modest amount of water with properly treated water and check why shrimp die.

Carbon filtering instead of a conditioner

Some keepers filter tap water through a carbon block or catalytic carbon cartridge. It only works if the cartridge is rated for chloramine, the water flows slowly enough for contact time, and the cartridge is replaced on schedule. Ordinary aquarium carbon in a tank filter is not a substitute. If you are unsure, a conditioner is the more forgiving method.

Questions we get asked

Is chloramine bad for shrimp?

Yes. Chloramine, a chlorine and ammonia compound, is toxic to shrimp and to the beneficial bacteria in a filter. Untreated tap water is a common cause of sudden losses after a water change.

Can I let tap water sit out to remove chloramine?

No, not in any practical time. Unlike free chlorine, chloramine does not leave the water by evaporation or aeration, so a bucket left standing still contains it. Treat the water instead.

Does San Diego tap water have chloramine?

Yes. The City of San Diego disinfects with chloramine, and its 2025 water quality report lists an average residual of about 1.75 ppm. Treat every batch of tap water before it reaches your shrimp.

Why does my test show ammonia after I treat the water?

A conditioner that neutralizes chloramine breaks it apart and frees the ammonia it carried. Some conditioners temporarily bind that ammonia, and some test kits can still read it. In a cycled tank the bacteria process it, but a high reading with sick shrimp deserves a large, careful water change using treated water.

Will a carbon filter remove chloramine?

Only if it is the right kind. Ordinary aquarium carbon removes it poorly. Carbon blocks or catalytic carbon rated for chloramine can work with enough contact time, so check the rating and replace it on the maker's schedule.

Can I put conditioner straight into the tank instead of the bucket?

It can work in an emergency, but treating the new water first is safer, because the shrimp are then never exposed to untreated water. Dose for the volume of water you are adding, following the product label, and do not overdose.
