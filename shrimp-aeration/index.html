Care guide

Do shrimp need an air stone?

Shrimp need oxygen-rich water. A rippling surface delivers it; an air stone is a useful backup, not a requirement.

The short answer

In one line

Keep the water surface gently moving and the tank from running hot or overstocked; add an air stone if your tank is warm, crowded or has no other source of surface movement.

Oxygen enters an aquarium mostly at the surface, where the water meets the air. Movement there, from a filter outflow or a bubble stream, keeps fresh oxygen dissolving in and carbon dioxide leaving. A calm, still surface in a full tank is where problems start.

Why filters often cover it

A sponge filter driven by an air pump does two jobs at once: it filters and it aerates, which is why it is a favourite for shrimp, see filters for shrimp tanks. A hang-on-back or canister outflow that ripples the surface also does the job. If your filter outflow is aimed at the glass or kept deep below the surface, the water may be well mixed but poorly aerated.

When extra aeration helps

Warm weather. Warmer water holds less oxygen while shrimp use more, so a summer heat spike is the classic time for trouble. Read shrimp tank temperature for the range to aim for.

Heavy stocking or lots of feeding. More animals and more decaying food both draw down oxygen, see how many shrimp per gallon.

Medications or filter downtime. If you turn the filter off while treating or cleaning, a small air pump keeps water moving. Many treatments are dangerous to shrimp in any case, see medications and copper.

Transport and acclimation. Shrimp that are bagged or sitting in a bucket are in still water, so work briskly and keep the container's surface area wide, see how to acclimate shrimp.

Signs of low oxygen

Shrimp gathered near the surface or at the filter outflow, unusual lethargy, and deaths that follow a hot day or a power cut can all point to low oxygen. These signs overlap with ammonia, nitrite and chemical exposure, so test the water before deciding, see why shrimp die. If you suspect low oxygen, increase surface movement right away and cool the tank gradually if it is hot.

Keeping it gentle

Shrimp do best without a violent current. Choose a smaller air stone or a lower pump setting, and shield intakes with a sponge so baby shrimp are not pulled in.

Questions we get asked

Do shrimp need an air stone?

Not always. What shrimp need is dissolved oxygen, and a filter that ripples the surface usually provides it in a lightly stocked, planted tank. An air stone is a cheap extra that helps in warm, heavily stocked or unplanted tanks, and when a filter is turned off.

How can I tell if my shrimp tank has low oxygen?

Shrimp clustering near the surface or at the filter outflow, sluggish or gasping behaviour, and sudden deaths in a warm or crowded tank can all point to low oxygen. Other problems such as ammonia or a chemical exposure look similar, so test the water too.

Does warm water hold less oxygen?

Yes. Water holds less dissolved oxygen as it gets warmer, and warm shrimp also use more of it. That is one reason a hot summer tank deserves extra surface movement; see the shrimp tank temperature guide.

Can an air stone hurt shrimp?

The bubbles themselves do not harm adult shrimp. A strong stream can stress them or sweep baby shrimp around, so keep the flow gentle and use a sponge filter or a baffled outflow if the tank has shrimplets.

Do live plants replace an air stone?

Plants release oxygen while the light is on but use some at night, so they are not a reliable substitute. Treat them as a bonus and keep the surface moving as well.
