RO water for shrimp: remineralizing for Caridina

Caridina need soft, low-TDS water, and in San Diego that means starting from RO and adding minerals back in a controlled way.

The short answer

In one line

RO water is a blank slate, not shrimp water. Remineralize it with a shrimp product to a steady target TDS, check it with a meter, and match the tank before it goes in.

San Diego tap water is hard, with a TDS of roughly 530 to 590 ppm and a pH around 8.3 in the City’s 2025 report. That suits Neocaridina once it is dechlorinated, but Caridina such as crystal reds want a pH of about 5.8 to 6.5 and a TDS of about 100 to 130 ppm, so they are set up on RO water instead. See Caridina care and San Diego tap water for shrimp.

Why not just use RO water as it comes?

A reverse osmosis unit removes nearly all dissolved minerals, so the water that comes out reads close to zero on a TDS meter. Shrimp need minerals to harden a new shell after each molt, so pure RO water can cause trouble, including failed molts. The goal is to add back only the minerals shrimp need, in a known amount.

How to remineralize

Choose the right product. Remineralizers are sold for different jobs. A GH-only product is the usual choice for Caridina over buffering soil, because the soil handles carbonate hardness. A GH plus KH product is aimed at Neocaridina and other shrimp that do not use acidic soil. Using the wrong one is a common cause of a tank that will not hold the right pH.

Measure, then mix. Fill a clean container with RO water, add the product according to its label, stir until it dissolves and read the TDS. If the number is below your target, add a little more and read it again. If it overshoots, dilute with more RO water. Write down the final recipe so every batch matches.

Match the tank. Bring the new water to the same temperature and TDS as the tank before using it, and add it slowly during a small water change. Large swings in TDS stress shrimp, so keep batches consistent rather than chasing a perfect number.

Mix ahead. Mixing a batch the day before lets the minerals dissolve fully and lets you confirm the reading. Store it in a clean, covered food-safe container.

Common mistakes

Using plain RO water with no minerals; using a GH plus KH product on a Caridina tank over buffering soil; trusting a TDS meter that has never been calibrated; topping up evaporation with remineralized water instead of plain RO, which slowly raises the TDS because minerals stay behind when water evaporates; and old RO filter stages that let chlorine or chloramine through. Check your RO unit’s stages on the maker’s schedule.

Compare your readings with the species ranges on water parameters, and review substrate and cycling before adding Caridina to a new tank.

Questions we get asked

Can shrimp live in plain RO water?

No. Pure RO water has almost no minerals, and shrimp need them to build new shells when they molt and to keep their body chemistry stable. RO water is a blank starting point that you remineralize with a product made for shrimp before it goes in the tank.

What TDS should remineralized RO water be for Caridina?

For crystal reds and other Caridina on buffering soil we aim for a TDS of about 100 to 130 ppm, a pH of about 5.8 to 6.5 and a temperature of about 68 to 74 F. Mix to the number the product label gives, check it with a TDS meter, and match the tank before you add it.

Can I use RO water for Neocaridina?

You can, but you do not need to in San Diego. Neocaridina tolerate dechlorinated tap water, and they usually do better on a GH plus KH remineralizer than on a GH-only one if you do choose to use RO. Our guide to San Diego tap water explains which species need what.

Does a TDS meter tell me the GH?

No. A TDS meter measures total dissolved solids, which includes more than the hardness minerals. It is useful for checking that each batch is mixed the same way, but test GH and KH with a drop test kit to see the actual hardness.

Do I need to dechlorinate RO water?

Water that has passed through a working RO unit has had most chlorine and chloramine removed, but the filter stages wear out. If your RO water comes from a unit whose carbon stages are old, or you are unsure, check with the supplier or treat it with a dechlorinator. Fresh, correctly maintained filters are the safer route.
